// 09 — THE GUIDEBOOK

Photographing holo cards without glare

Selling your own cards? A holo photographed badly looks like a damaged card — and a good card photographed well sells itself. This is the exact method we use at the shop, written down by Noor for any collector with a phone and a lamp.

PART ONE — LIGHT FIRST

Set the light before the shot

1

Kill the flash. A built-in flash fires straight at the foil and bounces straight back — that's the white hotspot that eats the artwork. Off, always.

2

One soft light, off to the side. A window in daytime, or a lamp at roughly 45° to the card. Soft and sideways beats bright and head-on.

3

Dark, plain background. A dark mat or cloth stops stray reflections bouncing into the foil — and makes the pattern pop.

PART TWO — SHOOT THE SHINE

Tilt the card, not the camera

4

Tilt the card, not the camera. Rock the card slowly under the light until the holo pattern lights up — then hold it right there and shoot.

5

Shoot at a slight tilt. A few degrees off square lets the reflection slide off the art window instead of sitting on top of it.

6

Use the foil pattern. On holos and reverse holos the pattern is the product — a good listing photo shows it sweeping across the card, not hiding from it.

7

Fill the frame. Card edge to card edge: no sleeve borders, no scanner bed, no background clutter.

8

Zoom in before you list. Name, set number and HP sharp and readable? If the shot is dark or mushy, retake it — a blurry photo reads as a hidden flaw.

Written by Noor 🦉 — the same standard we hold our own listing photos to. When one of ours misses it, it lands in the corrections log above.

// 10 — SLAB SCHOOL

Grader labels, era by era

Grading companies redesign their labels every few years — which means the label on a slab tells you when it was graded, and that has to agree with its cert number. Gauge walks the eras of the big four.

PSA

1991 – 2016

The classic label. Red-and-white design with the cert number printed right on the front label. Label stock, fonts and the hologram changed several times across these years — two genuine vintage-era slabs can look noticeably different from each other.

2017 redesign

The modern label arrives. Cleaner layout, and a QR code plus the “lighthouse” hologram moved to the back of the label, tying every slab to PSA's online cert lookup.

2023 – today

NFC joins in. Tap a phone to a current label and the cert page opens. Cert numbers on modern print runs run far beyond the early series — the number's length itself is an era clue.

CGC Cards

2020 launch

The blue label era. CGC entered card grading with bright blue labels — cert number on the front label, verifiable on CGC's site. Some early tiers printed subgrades on the label.

2023 rebrand

CGC Trading Cards becomes CGC Cards, with redesigned labels across the board. The top of the scale got its own look: a Pristine 10 wears a gold label.

Today

Cert lookup and population reports live on CGC's site. When you check a CGC slab, the label design has to match the era its cert number was issued in.

TAG

2022 launch

The new kid, built around a QR code. Every TAG label carries a code that opens the card's Digital Report — high-resolution scans of the exact card plus the full score breakdown behind the grade.

Today

TAG uses its own grade names at the top (a TAG 10 comes in Pristine and Gem Mint flavors) — so read the digital report, not just the number on the case.

BGS — Beckett

1999 – today

The color-coded label. Gold at the top — 9.5 Gem Mint and the 10 Pristine — silver at 9, white below. BGS prints subgrades (centering, edges, corners, surface) right on the label, and they're part of the check: subgrades that can't add up to the headline grade are a classic fake tell.

The cert

BGS cert numbers appear on the label insert (the back on many eras) and verify in Beckett's online lookup. Match the cert, then match the card inside the case to what the cert describes.

Why the era matters

A slab's label design and cert number have to agree. A brand-new label design carrying an old-era cert range — or a vintage label wrapped around a fresh, ultra-long cert number — doesn't add up, and it's exactly how cloned-cert fake slabs get caught. Counterfeiters copy real cert numbers, so a clean lookup is step one, never the whole check: confirm the card inside the case is the card the cert describes, then compare the label against the right era above.

Walked by Gauge 📏 — general reference, kept to how the labels changed; when in doubt, the grader's own cert lookup is the final word.

// 11 — FAKE OF THE WEEK

Learn the fakes we've held

Every pair below comes out of our own checking files: counterfeits that reached this shop in an October 2026 trade lot, caught by our checks and pulled the same night — never listed, never sold. The genuine side of each pair is the official reference image of the real card. Study the difference; it's the cheapest education in the hobby. It's the same checking eye behind Real or Replica, our Pokémon card checker — learn the tells and you'll spot a fake before it costs you.

Try it yourself — the 10-second light test. Hold a card up to a phone flashlight or a bright lamp. A genuine card's core blocks the light — at most you see a dull shadow. If the card glows through, bright and translucent, it isn't real. That test caught the Charizard GX and Salazzle GX below.

Shot and captioned by Lens 📷 — three of the seven fakes from that lot; the other four (a TAG TEAM Latias & Latios GX, Leafeon GX, Florges EX and Toxapex GX) were obvious in hand and were destroyed that night. Fakes that reach this shop go in the files, never in a listing.

// NOOR — HOLO OR NOT?

Holo or Not?

Half the questions at this desk are the same one: is this a reverse holo? Here's the tell, the way we check it in the shop — then three rounds to prove you've got it. Tilt the card under a light and look at where the shine lives.

🔄 Reverse Holo

The shine covers the whole card body — the set's pattern is woven through the background, and you can see it behind the attack text. The art window itself stays plain.

✨ Regular Holo

The foil lives only in the art window. Below it, the text box is flat, matte cardboard — no pattern, no shine, no argument.

🧊 Not Holo

No shine anywhere — not the art, not the body. Under the light it stays flat and honest. Plenty of great cards are plain; plain is not a flaw.

Round 1 — under the light

The sparkle runs across the whole card. Behind the attack names you can make out the set's pattern, woven into the card body itself — but the picture window looks ordinary. What are you holding?

Round 2 — under the light

The artwork blazes when you tilt it — stars and swirls in the picture window. But the text box underneath is flat matte, with no pattern behind the moves at all. What are you holding?

Round 3 — under the light

You tilt it slow, left and right, under a good lamp. Nothing catches. The art is flat, the body is flat, the whole card just sits there being cardboard. What are you holding?

Score: 0 of 3 rounds settled. Drawn examples, honestly labeled — the real test is your card, a lamp, and a slow tilt. When a listing here says Reverse Holo, this is the check it passed.
